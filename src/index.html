Try a fair test

  1. Measure the temperature of a hot asphalt tile.
  2. Plant a tree just west of it. Measure again.
  3. Swap a low building for a tall one. Whose shadow cools more?

These are classroom estimates of surface temperature, the heat you'd feel touching the ground. They aren't air temperatures or a weather forecast.

How it works

Materials heat differently

Dark surfaces absorb more sunlight. Reflective roofs and light surfaces stay cooler.

Shade blocks sunlight

Buildings and trees shade the tiles to their east. Taller buildings cast longer shadows, but their roofs can still be hot.

Trees cool their neighbors

Leaves release water, which cools nearby surfaces. The effect fades with distance and has a limit, so stacking trees can't cool forever.

Real temperatures also depend on wind, moisture, time of day, and local conditions.

Science sources & model limits

EPA explains urban heat islands, tree shade and water release, and reflective roofs. These sources support the ideas, not our exact temperature numbers.

All numerical parameters are illustrative. We hold the weather fixed, simplify material properties, and leave out wind, changing moisture, nighttime heat, and energy use. Displayed decimals help compare changes; they do not mean measured accuracy.
